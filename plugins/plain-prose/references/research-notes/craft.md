# Craft sources for rewriting scientific prose

Method. I read the text of each source in section 6, except S23 and S24, which I did not open. A line that says "only" names the part that I read. T1 means a study that I opened, with numbers that I read. T2 means a named guide or article states the rule and gives no measurement. UNVERIFIED means I did not open the page, or I saw only a snippet or a summary. Keys S1 to S24 map to URLs in section 6. Most T1 sources here count how often a pattern occurs. Few test whether a rewrite helps a reader, and section 5 lists those gaps. In section 3, the text `&mdash;` stands for the em dash character, so that this file holds none.

## 1. Principles (table)

| ID | Principle | What to do on the page | Tier, design, source key |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | Keep the subject next to its verb | Move a long insertion out of the gap between the subject and the verb. Put it before the subject or in a new sentence. | T2, expert essay with worked rewrites, S1; S5 |
| P2 | End the sentence on the new point | Read the last words of the sentence. Move any word that is not the main point away from the end. | T2, expert essay, S1 |
| P3 | Start from what the reader already knows | Put the term from the previous sentence in the subject slot. Then add the new fact. | T2, expert essay, S1 |
| P4 | One point per sentence and per paragraph | Split a sentence that holds two claims. A sentence is too long when more points compete for the end position than the end can hold. | T2, expert essay, S1; guide, S4 |
| P5 | Put the action in the verb | Turn a noun built from a verb back into the verb. Name the actor when the actor matters. | T2, essay and guides, S1; S4; S7 |
| P6 | Use one name for one thing | Repeat the term. A synonym makes the reader ask whether the meaning changed. | T2, guides, S2; S5 |
| P7 | Keep acronyms few and defined | Spell out an acronym that appears rarely. Keep at most two or three per document and define each at first use. | T2, guide, S4. T1, 24 million titles and 18 million abstracts, S12 |
| P8 | Replace general science words with plain words and keep technical terms | Swap the style word for the plain word with the same meaning. Leave terms of art in place. | T1, 709,577 abstracts, S3. T1, 15.1 million abstracts, S9. T2, guide, S4 |
| P9 | Replace praise with the measured fact | Move the number, the effect size or the citation into the sentence. Delete the praise word. | T1, PubMed abstracts 1974 to 2014, S11. T2, guideline, S8 |
| P10 | Name the source or drop the attribution | Cite the study. If no source exists, state the claim as your own or delete it. | T2, guideline, S8 |
| P11 | Delete words and sentences that do no work | Test each word and each sentence. Delete the ones that carry no claim, number or citation. | T2, guides, S5; S6. T1, 8,035 expert edits, S18. UNVERIFIED, S24 |
| P12 | State the point directly | Replace a roadmap sentence with the claim. A Results paragraph may open with the question it answers. | T2, essay and editorial, S6; S2 |
| P13 | Use context, content, conclusion at each scale | Open with context, give the content, close with the conclusion, in the paper, the section and the paragraph. | T2, editorial, S2 |
| P14 | Say what is, and keep negation for real contrasts | Rewrite a negated clause as a positive clause. Keep the negation when a test excluded the alternative. | T2, guide, S4 |
| P15 | Hedge once, at the claim, and give the size of the effect | Keep one hedge where the data leave real doubt. State the magnitude and the certainty when the paragraph holds them. | T2, essay, S6. T1, three corpora, S13; S14; S15 |
| P16 | Keep tense, quantifiers and scope | Keep past tense for results. Keep "in 14 of 20 mice" as written. Do not add action advice. | T1, 4,900 summaries, S17 |
| P17 | Make the smallest edit that fixes the fault | Change the words that cause the fault and leave the rest. A model edit moves the text far more than a human edit does. | T1, essay edits, S19. T1, expert edits, S18 |
| P18 | Keep citations, dates and names | Check that every citation, date and name in the original survives in the rewrite. | T1, 3,000 rewritten passages, S20 |
| P19 | Join or split sentences by their points | Test a long sentence by counting its competing points. Do not apply a fixed word limit. | T2, expert essay, S1. T1, S3 gives the limits of formula scores |
| P20 | Do not use a readability score as the goal | Use the score as a rough check only. A score gain does not show that readers understand more. | T1, 709,577 abstracts, S3 |

## 2. Rewrite strategy by family (table)

| Family | Strategy (one sentence) | Delete when | The rewrite must keep | Tier | Source key |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vocabulary with measured excess | Replace the style word with the plain word of the same meaning, or with the fact the word stands for. | The word adds no meaning, as `intricate` before "link". | Terms of art such as `robust standard errors`, `pivotal trial`, `genomic landscape`; numbers; hedges. | T2 for the rewrite; T1 for the excess | S4; S3; S9 |
| Figurative load words | Replace the figure with the literal term for the relation that the figure names. | The figure only decorates a sentence that already has a plain predicate. | The strength of the claim: "central to" must not become "required for". | T2 | S5; S7; S18 |
| Binary contrast | State the positive claim once. Drop the denied half unless a reader holds that view or a test addressed it. | No reader holds the denied view and no test addressed it. | A tested alternative with its result, its number and its figure reference. | T2 | S4; S1 |
| Importance puffery | Replace the praise with the measured result or the cited fact that supports it. | The paragraph holds no result that the praise could summarize. | The number, the unit and the citation. Keep praise that a cited source states. | T2 for the rule; T1 for the trend | S8; S11 |
| Trailing participial benefit clause | Move the clause into its own sentence with a subject and a verb. Delete it when it states a benefit that the data do not show. | The clause states an unmeasured benefit or implication. | Any number or citation in the clause. Keep the hedge on an implication. | T2 for the rule; T1 for the rate | S1; S4; S10 |
| Weasel attribution | Name the source with a citation. With no source, state the claim as the author's own or delete it. | The claim is common knowledge in the field and needs no source. | The citation and the count of sources ("two studies" stays two). Never add a citation the author did not give. | T2 | S8 |
| Fake-strong verbs | Use the verb that names the action, or use "is". | The sentence repeats its own heading or caption. | The functional claim: `serves as` stays when it states a role. S1's own revision uses "serve both to ... and to ...". Keep tense and subject. | T2 | S1; S4; S7 |
| Throat-clearing and signposting | Delete the opener and start with the claim. | The sentence only announces the next sentence or the section. | Any hedge, number or citation in the sentence. Keep the sentence that states the question a Results paragraph answers. | T2 | S6; S2; S5 |
| Recap openers | Delete the opener word and keep the statement when it draws a new inference. | The sentence repeats the previous sentence and draws no new inference. | The hedge and the numbers. Keep the summary that opens a Discussion. | T2 | S2; S4; S5 |
| Em dash | Choose the mark by function: commas or parentheses for an aside, a period or semicolon for a second clause, a colon for a list. | The aside holds no datum and the sentence is complete without it. | The names and numbers inside the aside. Keep one em dash per paragraph where it helps. | T2, indirect | S1 |
| Colon reveal | State the finding as the main clause. | The lead-in ("The result was clear") only announces. | Colons before lists, equations and definitions. | T2 | S1 |
| Rhetorical question answered at once | State the answer as a statement. | Always: delete the question and keep the answer. | A question that opens a Results paragraph or states a tested hypothesis. | T2 | S6; S2 |
| Assistant residue | Delete the whole span and tell the author to check the sentences that followed it. | Always. | Nothing from the span. Flag any number that followed, so the author can check its source. | T1, artifact only | S10 |
| Repeated sentence openers | Merge the sentences, or put the old information first so that each opener differs for a reason. | A sentence repeats a neighbor. | Parallel form for parallel claims. Keep "We" in Methods. | T2 | S1; S2 |
| Staccato run | Join three or more fragments into one or two sentences. Put the new fact last. | A fragment repeats a neighbor. | The numbers. Keep a short sentence that states a main finding or one Methods step. | T2 | S1; S4 |

## 4. Hedges: which to keep

What the sources say.

- Hedges are common in science. S14 finds a hedge in 13.39% of 6,383 clinical sentences, 17.70% of 11,871 abstract sentences and 19.44% of 2,670 full-paper sentences. S15 counts 7.86 hedges per 1000 words in 147,189 words of English medical articles. The range by specialty is 6.09 to 9.30.
- Authors place hedges where they interpret. In 100 gene-regulation abstracts, S13 finds speculation in 57 of 100 final sentences and in 45 of 693 earlier sentences. S15 also finds more hedges in the conclusion and results areas.
- A word list cannot find hedges. S14 reports that "or" marks speculation in 8.85% of its occurrences in abstracts. For some candidate cue words, up to 90% of occurrences are not hedges.
- S6 calls hedges used as a habit fluff and lists "somewhat", "sort of", "to some extent" and "I would argue". He asks writers to state the size of the effect and the degree of certainty. He accepts a hedge that is "a choice, not a tic".
- In five experiments (n = 5,780), readers saw more uncertainty and trusted the numbers and the source slightly less, mostly for verbal wording (S16). The readers were lay readers of news facts.
- Dropping qualifiers changes meaning. Three models summarizing science papers overgeneralized in 26% to 73% of cases (S17). Model summaries of 100 articles were 4.85 times as likely as expert summaries to hold a broad generalization (95% CI 3.06 to 7.70). A prompt that asked for accuracy made it worse (OR 1.90, 95% CI 1.11 to 3.26).
- One professional editor of creative text advises: write the verb without "seem to" unless the text has a specific doubt (S18).
- Hyland describes content-oriented and reader-oriented hedges. I know this only from secondary descriptions, so it is UNVERIFIED (S23).

The rule the plugin should apply.

1. Never flag a hedge from a word list alone. A list matches non-hedges too often (S14).
2. Keep a hedge that qualifies an inference, a causal claim from an observational design, or a result beyond the sample. Keep it in Discussion sentences and in the last sentences of an abstract.
3. Propose an edit for stacked hedges, such as "may possibly suggest". Keep the one hedge that the data support. Ask the author when the choice is unclear.
4. Replace a vague hedge on a measured quantity with the number and interval only when the paragraph already holds them. Otherwise keep the hedge and ask the author (S6).
5. Never change a past-tense result to present tense. Never drop a quantifier or add a recommendation (S17). This overrides the plain-language rule to prefer present tense (S4).
6. Add a script guard `hedge-kept`. Count a short closed list of epistemic cues in `before` and `after`. The counts must match unless the proposal states a reason. A false match makes the guard fail safe.
7. Give hedges no density flag. Report the rate per 1000 words as `info` only. The one corpus I opened with a manual count gives 6.09 to 9.30 per 1000 words (S15).

## 5. What the evidence does not show

- No source tests whether a given rewrite helps readers of scientific prose. S1 gives principles with worked rewrites and no reader test. S3 cites a study (Hartley, 1994) in which rewritten abstracts improved comprehension. I did not open it, so I do not use it.
- Most T1 sources count patterns (S3, S9, S10, S11, S12). None measures whether removing a word changes comprehension or credibility.
- S3 does not test acronyms. Its preprocessing removed abbreviations. S12 measures acronym use only and has no comprehension data. Its abstract calls acronyms "not the biggest current problem".
- S10 measures present participial clauses in general, on text continued by models across six genres. It does not measure the trailing benefit clause or manuscripts. I did not read its split by genre.
- S18 uses creative paragraphs. Its figure for meaning-preserving edits comes from a similarity score with a threshold of 0.6, and the authors call the boundary a continuum. S19 uses argumentative essays and conference reviews. S21 uses essays. S22 uses personal-style tasks.
- Reader preference is mixed. In S20, 20 NLP researchers preferred model-improved passages for clarity (mean -0.44, d = -0.38, 200 rated pairs, a pilot). In S18, writers ranked writer-edited text first (mean rank 1.53) and unedited model text last (2.47 to 2.51). The domains and tasks differ. No study I opened tests a detect, propose and approve flow.
- No study I opened tests whether removing a hedge changes a peer reader's judgment. S16 tests lay readers of news facts. S23 is UNVERIFIED, and no number from it appears in this note.
- Binary contrast, colon reveal, rhetorical question, repeated openers and staccato run have no measured source. Their rewrite rules rest on T2 guides (S1, S2, S4, S6). S1 itself says that any reader expectation can be violated to good effect.
- No source I opened gives a rewrite rule for the em dash. The strategy derives from S1 on colons and semicolons. S1 allows a colon after a full sentence, so the colon family should flag only the announce-then-reveal shape.
- S6 recommends a question in place of a signpost, and S2 shows questions opening Results paragraphs. The rhetorical-question family must keep those uses.
- S4 asks for short sentences. S1 rejects word limits. The staccato strategy follows S1 and applies to three or more fragments only.
- S8 is an encyclopedia guideline. Use it for attribution and praise only.
- I did not open these books: Zinsser, "On Writing Well" (S24, UNVERIFIED); Sword, "Stylish Academic Writing"; Pinker, "The Sense of Style"; and Schimel, "Writing Science". S2 cites Schimel for patient and impatient readers.
- I read S1 from a 2011 copy of a Penn State reprint, through the Internet Archive. The American Scientist page returned HTTP 503. I read S6 from a reproduction of the Chronicle essay. S9, S10, S17 and S18 to S22 are preprints, and I read them as preprints.
