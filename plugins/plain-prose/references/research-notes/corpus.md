# Corpus evidence for LLM-style markers in scientific text

Method. I downloaded each paper as a PDF (or read the arXiv HTML) and read the text with pdftotext. I read the full main text of S1, S3 to S10, S13 to S16 and the cited parts of S11, S12, S17, S21 and S25. I read Tables 2 and 3 of S4 and Figure 2 of S3 from rendered pages. For S1 I also downloaded the authors' released counts (S2) and recomputed r and the frequency gap d of every word with the formulas of the paper. The recomputation reproduces S1: 454 excess words in 2024, 379 style words, the 291 words of its Figure S6, r = 28.0, 13.8 and 10.7 for delves, underscores and showcasing, and d = 0.052, 0.041 and 0.037 for potential, findings and crucial. The printed excess threshold has a sign that gives 26,385 words if read literally. The reversed sign gives 454 words, so I use it.

- T1: a controlled or corpus study that I opened and whose numbers I read.
- T2: a named source states the item and gives no measurement.
- UNVERIFIED: I did not open the page, or I saw only a snippet or a summary.

Keys S1 to S27 map to URLs in section 4. In S1 rows, r is the 2024 share of abstracts that contain the word divided by the share extrapolated from 2021 and 2022. The gap d is the difference of the two shares. The value 1 - 1/r is my own arithmetic. It gives the share of 2024 abstracts with the word that exceed the extrapolation (0.96 for r = 28, 0.50 for r = 2, 0.23 for r = 1.3). It describes a corpus. It does not describe one document. Word forms are the forms the papers counted, in lower case.

## 2. Em dash and punctuation

- P1 (T1, S6). Share of Discussion sections with at least one em dash: 4.23% (1,368 of 32,324) before 30 November 2022 and 11.58% (4,322 of 37,308) after. Difference +7.35 points (95% CI 6.94 to 7.77), odds ratio 2.96 (2.77 to 3.17). Corpus: 69,632 first-version medRxiv preprints of 2020 to 2025 with a Discussion of 500 characters or more. The count is of the literal character U+2014 in the JATS XML.
- P2 (T1, S6). By year: 4.00% (2020), 4.61%, 4.00%, 4.42% (2023), 8.03% (2024), 20.30% (2025), 23.5% in the third quarter of 2025. Length-adjusted rate ratio 3.33 (3.05 to 3.63). At abstract level the odds ratio is 2.92 (2.60 to 3.27). Placebo split inside the pre-LLM years: +0.13 points (CI -0.33 to +0.58). Acknowledgments: 0.22% to 0.46%. Exploratory: in 2025, 32.1% of preprints that declare AI use have an em dash in the Discussion, against 19.9% of the others.
- P3 (stated limits of S6). The em dash is a population marker and not a per-paper detector. The measure is presence, not count. One preprint server. No clean typographic control, because common punctuation drifts upward. First versions only, and the XML is a conversion of the submitted PDF. The paper makes no causal claim. It is a preprint. About 4% of Discussions held an em dash before the LLM era, so one em dash in a section is common in human text.
- P4 (T1, S13). CHI full papers: dash punctuation counted as one family (em dash, en dash, double hyphen, spaced hyphen between words) stayed between 0.68 and 1.41 per 1,000 tokens from 1982 to 2025. It was 1.21 in 2025 and 2.08 in 2026. The 2026 value rests on one edition and the authors call it provisional. The em dash glyph alone is unreliable in the scanned volumes, because the encoding changed around 2001. This is the only per-1,000-token baseline I found. It is not an em-dash-only rate.
- P5 (T2, S19). A blog post compared 10,000 ecology abstracts from OpenAlex for 2021 and 2025. It reports that the em dash frequency per character "more than doubled" and that no other character came close. Question marks, ampersands and semicolons fell. The post gives no table. S6 warns that OpenAlex does not always keep the character.
- P6 (T1, S21). PubMed's abstract field kept typographic punctuation in only 0.6% of abstracts (95% CI 0.3 to 1.0), in a sample of 4,000 PMC articles of 2024. A study that takes abstracts from PubMed cannot see em dashes. S1, S5 and S8 count words, so the loss does not affect their word numbers. I read the abstract and the introduction.
- P7 (T2, S20). The page says LLMs overuse em dashes in formulaic places, usually with spaces around them, and that the sign is "most useful when taken in combination". It says some newer models suppress em dashes and cites a July 2026 study that I did not find or open. I did not verify this.
- P8 (not given by any source). No density threshold for science text. No data for the LaTeX forms "---" and "--" or for the en dash. No data for the colon and the semicolon as markers. The thresholds for the plugin need the author's voice samples.

## 3. What the evidence does not show

1. No per-document test. S1 says its method cannot identify single abstracts. S5 says its evidence does not support authorship attribution of one abstract. S6 calls the em dash a population indicator. No source gives precision or recall of a word list on single documents. A hit of the plugin is a style flag and carries no claim about authorship.
2. Excess is not cause. S1 says it cannot separate direct LLM use from authors who copy LLM words. S10 assumes that all excess comes from LLMs and says other causes exist. S6 makes no causal claim. Domain drift was checked in S1 (K11). The label style or content is a manual choice. Some "style" words are generic academic words that a topic shift can move (findings, approach, impact, role, techniques, research, outcomes, strategies, conditions). S5 removed such words from its set.
3. Lists age. In S14, delve and intricate fell in arXiv abstracts from April 2024 while significant and additionally kept rising. In S13, delve in CHI went from 0.64 (2024) to 0.06 (2026) per 10,000 tokens, and only 6 of 17 markers still doubled in 2026. In S10, 30 of 80 terms fell from 2024 to 2025. In S22, marker rates at NSF and UKRI fell in 2026 (short report, no word list). Two explanations are offered: authors avoid known words (S14) or newer models emit them less (S13, S22). Neither is tested. The T1 words describe 2023 and 2024 text. They are not measured for 2026 text.
4. Abstract versus full text. S1, S3, S7, S8 and S5 measure abstracts. S6 measures Discussion sections. Full-text numbers come from S9 and S25 (search counts), S10, S11, S12 and S13. K1 to K9 were found in abstracts. S10 finds that rankings differ between abstracts and full texts (T7). S12 finds that Discussion text has twice the estimated LLM use of Methods text. A list built on abstracts may fit Methods less well.
5. Venue, field and country. S1 reports gaps from 0.05 (UK, Australia) to 0.20 (China, South Korea, Taiwan) and 0.07 for Nature, Science, Cell. S4 found no change in Nature portfolio reviews. S10 uses one publisher, MDPI, which S1 places among the highest (0.21), so its ratios probably exceed those of the whole literature. S5 uses 28,415 abstracts with a cap of 5,000 a year and a small 2023 sample. S13 covers one field, and its growth ratios lie below the Scopus values.
6. Lower bound. The 13.5% of S1 is a lower bound. S12 (preprint) argues that frequency gaps underestimate use and reports 31% (abstracts) and 52% (full papers) for 2024. A text without these words can still come from an LLM.
7. Cause of the words. S8 found no evidence that training data, architecture or algorithm choices cause the words. A Llama 2 base versus chat comparison is consistent with fine-tuning or RLHF. The human experiment is inconclusive: for abstracts that open with delve, participants preferred the version without focal words (p = 0.023); for other items p = 0.651. S15 finds that base Llama 3 models match human rates and instruction-tuned models do not, and concludes that instruction tuning introduces the style. The words come from GPT-3.5 and GPT-4o models. S8 notes that GPT-4o mini no longer overuses boasts and uses underscore more.
8. Language. All rows are English. S1 notes that native speakers may remove such words, so the measure can miss careful use. S12 gives 0.28 for the UK and 0.85 for South Korea (2025). S23 (Korean, abstract only) reports marker turnover that follows model generations. Nothing here supports a list for another language.
9. Constructions not measured in science text. I found no study that measures "not X but Y", "not only ... but also", "less about X, more about Y", "it is not X, it is Y", "it is important to note", "plays a crucial role", "stands as a testament", "Importantly,", "In conclusion,", "Overall,", "Taken together,", the colon reveal or weasel attribution in published scientific text. S20 names several of them (W1 to W8) and gives no number. S5 holds single words only. S18 measures similar 5-grams on question-and-answer data. S24 measures repeated part-of-speech templates in generated text and does not name any of these constructions (I read the abstract and skimmed it).
10. Constructions measured outside science text. R1 to R3 come from mixed genres and 500-word continuations. B1 and B2 come from 75-document essay sets with LLM annotation. Le1 uses 50 abstracts per group and chatbots of 2023. None measures a trailing participial benefit clause or the list of three in a scientific manuscript. Tricolon in B1 cannot separate padding from real triples.
11. Evidence against two spec families. B2 finds fewer rhetorical questions in LLM text, so the spec family "rhetorical question answered at once" (T3) has no support and one measurement against it, in essays. G3 and R3 find fewer is, are and agentless passives in LLM text. Flagging passive voice or "is" has no support. X2 shows passive voice and hedges fall over decades without LLMs.
12. Words that fell or did not rise. In S10, thus, moreover, furthermore and importantly fell (T6). The spec family "Importantly," has no T1 support. The control word furthermore stayed flat in S9.
13. My numbers. The r and d of K1 to K9 are my recomputations from S2. They match the checkpoints in the Method paragraph. I rounded r to one decimal in K1 to K6 and two in K7 to K9. I copied the numbers of S4 to S10 and S13 to S18 from the tables or text. Values marked "read from plot" (L1, G2) are approximate.
14. Status of the sources. S1 is published in Science Advances and I read the arXiv version 5, which the arXiv page says reverts to version 3. I did not compare it with the journal text. S6, S10, S11, S12, S13, S16 and S25 are arXiv preprints, and I did not check for peer review. S5 is a journal article whose data are available only on request.
15. Wikipedia. S20 is an edited page. The statements on 2026 models and on a July 2026 study are claims of the page. I did not verify them.
