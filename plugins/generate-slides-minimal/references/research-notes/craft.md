# Slide craft: what the best-known presenters teach

Tiers: T1 = controlled study I opened. T2 = named author states it in a source I opened. T3 = repeated by others, no primary source opened. Source IDs (S1 to S23) map to the URLs in section 5. I read S4, S5, S21 and S23 only through a fetch summary; I read the rest as raw text.

Count: T1 = 2 (rows 4, 5); T2 = 12; T3 = 1 (row 7).

Limits of the T1 studies. Garner and Alley (S15) randomized 110 engineering undergraduates (59 versus 51) to an eight-minute narrated MRI talk. Assertion-evidence slides averaged 21.2 words; common-practice slides averaged 41.5. The first group scored higher on the essay (d = 0.81) and on a delayed test about a week later (d = 0.89), and reported lower mental effort (3.01 versus 3.61 of 7). Immediate factual recall did not differ. That group also rated its prior knowledge higher. Alley co-authored the study and promotes the method. Farkas (S16) calls the control slides poor. The study changed several features at once. Yue, Bjork and Bjork (S17) tested a narrated PowerPoint lesson (Experiment 1: 105 students). Text identical to the narration impaired learning; slightly different text helped.

No opened study tests the glance test, the 30-point minimum, the six-word cap, the sparkline, or the three-act story.

## 2. Agreement and disagreement

**Agreement (three or more authors).**
- One message per talk and per slide: Reynolds, Anderson, Duarte, Doumont, Atkinson.
- Slides support the speaker and do not replace speech: Anderson, Godin, Duarte, Reynolds, Doumont, Atkinson.
- Noise removal: Reynolds, Doumont, Godin, Tufte, Anderson.
- The audience comes first: Reynolds, Duarte, Atkinson, Doumont, Anderson.
- Detail belongs in a separate document: Reynolds, Godin, Duarte, Tufte.
- Content outranks design: Tufte (S3), Anderson (S2), Reynolds (S6).
- Tufte, Godin, Reynolds, Anderson and Alley criticize bullet slides, for different reasons. Tufte objects to low information per slide.

**Disagreement.** I report these as separate positions.
1. Text density. Tufte says a typical 40-word slide carries about eight seconds of reading and forces "relentless sequentiality" (S3). He wants rich detail shown side by side. Godin sets "No more than six words on a slide. EVER." (S1). Alley sits between at about 21 words plus one figure. This split is the sharpest.
2. Thirty-point type versus data slides. A 30-point minimum cannot hold a labelled scatter plot or Tufte's 196-number table. This conflict is my inference; I did not open Kawasaki's page.
3. Handouts. Godin: give nothing at the start and never print slides (S1). Duarte: for research findings, "send the full document to the audience in advance" (S2). Tufte favors detailed handouts (S23, summary level).
4. Sentence headlines. Alley requires them and a visual on each slide (S19). Farkas says they can hinder the speaker, forced visuals are often useless, and brief bullets are harmless (S16).
5. Slides at all. Anderson: many of the best TED speakers use none (S2).
6. Rules versus context. Godin and Kawasaki give hard numbers. Reynolds refuses formulas and answers "it depends" (S8).

## 3. Talk-level structure

- **Through-line.** Anderson frames the talk as a journey, often a detective story: a problem, a search, an "aha" (S2). Duarte defines a Big Idea as a point of view plus the stakes (S12). Atkinson starts from a change that creates an imbalance for the audience (S18). Reynolds suggests problem and solution (S6). Doumont adds a preview and review (S14).
- **Opening.** Reynolds: a surprising statistic or short story (S6). Anderson: introduce the topic fast, say why you care, and match the audience's knowledge (S2). Rosling: he opens with his students' test score on a child-mortality quiz (S13).
- **Number of ideas.** Reynolds: one core takeaway, one or two more later (S6). Anderson: limit the scope and go deeper (S2). Kawasaki: ten concepts at most (T3, S22).
- **Ending.** Reynolds and Duarte want an explicit call to action (S6, S12). Anderson says to let the audience draw conclusions (S2).
- **Length.** Anderson: 18 minutes or shorter at TED (S2). Reynolds: change pace about every 10 minutes, citing Medina (T3, S6).

## 4. Fit by audience

No source I opened tests these mappings. They are my judgment.

- **Talk to peers.** Use principles 1, 4, 5, 9, 11, 13. Peers inspect evidence, so a sentence headline over one figure suits them. Keep axes legible, and apply Tufte's side-by-side comparison. The T1 evidence used students, not peers.
- **Lab meeting.** Use principles 1, 4, 5, 11 and allow denser slides. The audience knows the context and the goal is feedback, so name the question you need answered. This mapping has the least support.
- **General audience.** Use principles 1 to 3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 14, 15. Anderson: assume less knowledge than you expect, avoid jargon, use examples (S2). The 30-point rule and the six-word cap fit best here.

For a tool: a rule checker can test principles 3 to 7. Principles 12 to 15 need a story planner. The authors conflict on density, so the tool needs an audience setting that changes the word budget.
